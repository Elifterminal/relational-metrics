What this project established

Consolidated after 43 experiments. Claims are sorted by what they rest on rather than by how interesting they are — that division is the most useful thing here, and conflating the two groups is how a project like this fools itself.

Six results come from exhaustive computation over enumerated systems. No annotation, no corpus, no human judgement enters them at any point; they are checked over every case in a finite space rather than sampled.

Three depend on somebody turning text into structures by hand — and this project spent a day establishing that the somebody was biased and that this step is the bottleneck. Those two groups deserve very different confidence.
What the whole thing amounts to: a working instrument with a hand-prepared input. The comparison engine does what it claims on formalised relational descriptions, confirmed by someone with no stake in the outcome. The step from text to structure is unsolved, was doing more of the work than the engine was, and on real prose appears not to be solvable in this representation at all.

Smaller than the goal, and genuinely a result — most projects that fail here never learn which half was broken.

How the tests work

The rules every experiment here follows, and why they exist. The Thesis tab is what the project is for; Tests & results is the running log.

A relational framework can relate anything to anything if the criteria are loose enough. That is the characteristic failure of this family of ideas, and it usually kills the project by succeeding — producing striking, plausible, well-visualised connections that are worthless. Everything below exists to make that failure detectable.

1. Six statements before anything runs

No experiment starts without these on paper. If any is missing the result isn't admissible.

#whatwhy
1Exact claimOne test, one sharply defined claim. Not "does this work" — "can it identify equivalent organisation when labels, coordinates and vocabulary all differ?"
2Intended signalThe specific feature that should cause success. If you can't name it, you can't tell success from luck.
3Rival explanationsEverything else that could produce the same result — listed before seeing any output.
4Positive controlA case where the signal is definitely there.
5Adversarial controlA case built to look convincing while lacking the signal.
6Falsification conditionThe specific result that would make us reject the idea — written down first, so it can't be renegotiated afterwards.

2. The condition matrix

Comparing two things is not one test, it is five.

surfacestructurerequired result
Asamesamehigh
Bdifferentsamehigh — can it see past vocabulary
Csamedifferentlow — is it just reading words
Ddifferentdifferentlow
Epersuasively similaralmost, critically different must locate the mismatch

B and C are the real tests. E matters most: two systems both containing feedback, where one settles and one runs away. A vague measure calls them identical. A useful one keeps them apart and says where.

3. The impostors

Before a measure is trusted, methods that cheat get built and the controls have to catch all of them: ones that read node names, count edges, compare densities or degree sequences, predict a constant, memorise the development set, or refit a parameter after seeing each pair. They aren't strawmen — each is a shortcut a real implementation could take by accident.

This has already paid for itself twice. The first version of the battery caught six of seven, and fixing the seventh is what exposed a defect in the real measure.

4. Evidence rungs

Every claim states its rung. They are not interchangeable, and nothing here is above rung 1.

rungmeans
1mathematically coherent — well-formed, behaves consistently
2recovers planted ground truth in synthetic worlds
3transfers across independently written generators
4works on natural data
5improves a real application against relevant baselines
6predicts something withheld
7supports a broader physical interpretation — not currently reachable

5. Rules learned the hard way

Every one of these was learned by publishing something wrong first. They are listed with the finding that produced them, because a rule without its scar is easy to forget.

rulelearned from
A controlled experiment is blind exactly where it controls. For every property a condition set holds fixed, keep one condition where it varies.size-matching hid a size bias in a measure and made an impostor undetectable
Never state a ground truth from inspection. Compute it, from the same object the measure sees.eight instances, and counting — the latest found an "unrelated" control that was isomorphic to its own query
Verify test cases are actually distinct before counting them as separate evidence — compare the objects, not the names.four "independent" agreements turned out to be two functions counted twice

6. What gets reported

  • Never a single number as the outcome — a profile, not a score.
  • Which rung, which controls survived, and which rival explanations are still live.
  • Failures written up with the same care as successes. Three of the results in this log are failures of the author's own proposals, and one is a retraction of a claim published on this page — left visible, with the original wording, rather than edited away.
  • The question is never "did it work". It is: which precise claim survived which controls, and what else could still explain this?

Stage 2 — can text become structure?

Chartered 2026-07-26, after 39 experiments. Written before any Stage 2 work began. Nothing in the charter gets edited after the first result — changes go in the log at the bottom, with the reason and the date.

Stage 1 asked: given two situations already written down as structures, can we tell a real cross-domain analogy from a convincing fake?

The answer is a qualified yes. Someone else, told nothing about the purpose, recovered the effect. My own annotation had been inflating it about two and a half times. The result is fragile at nine test cases where about thirty are needed.

What forced the shift

We tested the whole pipeline on real narrative text once, and got nothing. Not a weak signal — nothing.

And the diagnostic showed the comparison engine wasn't what failed. The annotation carried no signal at all: three cases favoured the right answer, thirteen showed no difference, four favoured the wrong one. Chance.

The sentences Stage 1 succeeded on look like this: "flow concentrates in a channel, deepens it, which draws more flow." That is most of the way to being a structure before anyone annotates it. Real documents are not like that.
So Stage 1 validated the second half of a two-stage pipeline, on inputs that were already nearly structured. The first half — turning text into a structure — has never been shown to work on anything else, and it's where the difficulty lives.

Stage 2 question: can text become structure automatically, well enough that the comparison still works?

What carries forward — and why this section exists

Stage 1's real output wasn't a formula. It was a set of habits that caught nine of my own errors, several of them after I'd published. Those are conditions of Stage 2, not aspirations, so they're written down rather than assumed:

Three rules Stage 2 adds, because Stage 1 earned them

1 — The annotator and the evaluator must be different parties, by construction. Stage 1's central defect was that I annotated the documents and judged the results. Every control after the benchmark failure was an attempt to unpick that. In Stage 2 annotation is automatic and I never touch it — my role is specifying the format and scoring the output.
2 — The annotator must be able to refuse, and its refusal rate is a reported number. The independent annotator declined eight of sixty documents. I never once returned an empty annotation across the same sixty — given a sentence with no causal claim in it, I invented one every time. That's the mechanism behind an edge I fabricated and then published a conclusion on. An annotator without an abstain option will manufacture structure.
3 — Natural text only. No tidy one-liners. Stage 1's documents were written by people who knew they were describing a causal structure. That's what made annotation easy and what makes the result unrepresentative.

The test bed, chosen before any result

A published benchmark of 254 items: real narratives, a genuine cross-domain analogy against a surface-similar decoy, answers set by other people.

234 remain untouched. Twenty were spent earlier and are burned — I've read them and can't un-read them, so they're excluded. Since about thirty are needed for a settled result, sample size is for once the least of the problems.

What would prove Stage 2 wrong

Written now, so it can't be softened later:
  1. Automatic annotation of real text produces no signal → the pipeline doesn't work on prose. Report it plainly. This is the most likely single outcome, and saying so in advance is the point.
  2. Signal exists but is weaker than hand annotation on the same documents → the automatic step is lossy; quantify how much and stop claiming more.
  3. The annotator abstains on more than half → the test didn't run. No claim either way.
  4. Signal appears only on documents I've previously seen → contamination, discard.

What Stage 2 does not attempt

A general theory of relational measurement — Stage 1 closed that off. Anything needing the four channels proved to be outside the representation: specificity, whether a node is real, timing, and provenance. And scale — everything stays small and exhaustively checkable until the prose question is answered.

Stage 2 findings

S2-1 — the representation isn't the blocker

Plan locked in version control before the experiment ran.

Deliberately a pre-test rather than an attempt. One of Stage 1's hardest-won rules is: before building a measure, try to prove it impossible. Stage 2 proposes an automatic reader for real prose — so first, can the representation even hold the difference between a genuine analogy and a decoy? If those two come out structurally identical, no reader can separate them and Stage 2 is over before it starts.

Structurally identical on zero of twenty items. The correct answer and the decoy differ in every single case. Dropping the relation types merges two of them, which means the types were carrying the whole distinction there — worth knowing when choosing what a reader should output.
And the known bias in this data works against the result, which is why it's usable. These annotations are mine, and I annotate to a four-relation template. A template flattens structures together — it can't push them apart. So a low identical-rate is trustworthy in a way a high one wouldn't have been. It survived a bias pointed the wrong way.
What that settles: the earlier failure on real narrative text was the reader, not the representation. Which matches, from a completely different direction, the finding that I was annotating to a template rather than to the text.
And the much smaller thing it actually shows. Two structures differing is not the same as differing in the direction that favours the right answer. The earlier failure included thirteen cases where structures differed but the scores tied. This clears one specific obstacle — the distinction being absent from the representation — and nothing else.

The locked plan says it in advance: this does not show that a good reader exists, only that the representation doesn't rule one out.
Three predictions, written before running, all three correct.

S2-2 — a rule-based reader cannot read prose

Failure predicted in writing before the run, and run anyway.

The first actual attempt. A fully automatic reader — not me, no minimum output, able to return nothing — pointed at thirty real narratives it had never seen.

It produced nothing at all for 69% of passages, and every one of the thirty items had to be dropped. The test didn't run. No claim either way about the measure.
My prediction was right about the outcome and wrong about the manner. I wrote down that it would fail by producing a signal indistinguishable from noise. What happened instead is that it produced almost nothing to measure. That distinction matters — "weak signal" and "no output" call for different next moves — so it's scored as directionally correct and specifically wrong.
What it's worth: a floor. Any reader tried next has to beat 69% blank, and that number now exists instead of being assumed. It also confirms the gap the pre-test implied — the representation can hold these analogies; what's missing is something able to recover them from prose.
And the thing I'm deliberately not doing. The obvious move is to keep improving the reader until it produces something. That is exactly the failure this project keeps catching. I've seen this benchmark — tuning a reader against data whose answers I know, until a signal appears, is the annotation bias in a new costume, and worse, because the target would be the result itself rather than the annotation.

The rule that the reader and the judge must be different parties applies to me iterating on a reader while watching the score, just as much as it applied to me annotating by hand. So the reader stays as it is.

S2-3 — why prose is hard, specifically

Measures properties of the text. No correct answer is read at any point, so nothing here can be steered by a score.

"Prose is harder" isn't actionable. So what does the difference actually consist of?

The difficulty isn't syntactic. Sentence complexity is the one thing that doesn't differ — narrative sentences are no more tangled than the glosses were.

It's that the causation is never written down. In these stories, 88% of sentences contain no causal word at all. The glosses were written to describe a causal structure, so they state it. Stories convey causation through sequence and consequence instead. A reader hunting for causal verbs finds nothing — which is precisely the 69% blank measured above.
So a reader has to do three things: infer causation that no word marks; follow a chain running across sentences through "he" and "it"; and turn Johnny and Kate into roles rather than people. None are available to a word-list and a clause splitter, which closes the question of why the last attempt failed. All three are things a language model does natively — which is why the next experiment is worth running rather than a formality.

It does not predict success. A reader capable of all three can still produce annotations carrying no signal. That has already happened once.
One planned check could not run. I said I would verify the gap isn't just "prose is longer" by comparing length-matched samples. Only two glosses fall in the overlap window against thirty-three passages, so there wasn't enough to test. Four of the five measures are already rates — per 100 words, per sentence — so length is divided out by construction, but that's an argument rather than the check I promised, and it's reported as untested rather than assumed.

Three predictions right, one wrong, and the wrong one is the finding

I predicted the reader would keep its ability to decline. On the tidy glosses it refused six times out of sixty — sensibly, on documents that genuinely said nothing.

On ninety narratives it refused zero times. Same instructions, same explicit permission.

A story never looks empty. There is always a sequence of events you can read as causation, so the safeguard never engages — precisely where it is needed most. The rule required the reader be able to refuse. It was able. The text never gave it a reason it could recognise.

The floor check added to the charter two findings ago is what caught this, on its first run.
Does that invalidate the negative? No — it points the same way. Manufactured structure is noise, not signal, so a floor makes an empty result more likely rather than less. The failure is real for this reader on this text.

But it doesn't separate two explanations: prose analogies may not reduce to these structures at all, or this reader may have manufactured plausible-looking structure on all ninety and buried the signal in it.

S2-5 — pursuing the one route left, and closing it

The failure had an identifiable cause: the reader never declined, so it manufactured structure everywhere. The obvious fix is to let it mark a relation as inferred rather than stated, so the invented material can be filtered out. That needs another commission — so first, cheaply, does the idea leave any trace in data already collected?

It doesn't. If invented relations were noise, items where the reader wrote fewer of them should carry more signal. The correlation is −0.11 with a p-value of 0.57 — the direction is right and the size is undetectable. The plan I'd locked said: on that basis, don't spend a commission.

Relation count is a crude stand-in for "how much was invented", and noticing that afterwards doesn't rescue the result. I chose it and named it as the test.

So I asked the sharper question instead

Not "how much did it write" but: does any subset of what it wrote carry signal? If some subset does, the material is there and filtering could find it. If none does, filtering cannot help however well it's built.

The trap is obvious and is the whole design. Search enough subsets and you will find one that looks good — that's guaranteed, not a risk. So the same search is run on data with the answers randomly flipped, and only the excess over that counts.

Cherry-picking on real data does worse than cherry-picking on randomised data. And the search isn't idle — it chose a strict subset on 21 of 30 items. There was plenty to select. Selecting just cannot help.

The signal is absent, not buried.
So that route is closed, and I said in advance I'd close it. The locked plan carried a commitment rather than a prediction: if this comes back negative I'll report the idea as dead rather than proposing a third variant of it. It came back negative. There's no fourth idea here, and reaching for one is exactly what that commitment existed to stop.

Stage 2's answer

This pipeline works on formalised descriptions and does not work on prose.
Four independent ways of asking, one answer. The representation can hold these analogies. A rule-based reader can't produce structure from prose. A competent reader produces structure that carries no signal. And that missing signal isn't hidden under invented noise.

These analogies don't survive being turned into typed relations between pairs of things. That's stronger and more specific than what Stage 2 started with — and it's negative. Two readers, plus exhaustive filtering of one of them, all give nothing.
Smaller than the goal. Real. Arrived at by a test that could not be adjusted after the fact, on data from outside the project, against answers nobody here set, and predicted in writing before the request went out. The charter named this as the most likely single outcome and said what to do with it. This is doing it.

Three readers have now been tried on the same narratives — a rule-based extractor that produced nothing, and a language model that produced everything and told us nothing. The gap between "can produce structure" and "produces the right structure" is the whole problem, and making the reader better at producing structure does not close it.

Stage 2 — closed

Closed 2026-07-26, with its central question answered. Not abandoned, not paused — answered, negatively, by a test that could not be adjusted after the fact.

What it asked, and what came back

Stage 2 asked one thing: can text become structure automatically, well enough that the comparison still works? Four independent ways of asking, one answer:

  • the representation can hold these analogies — right answer and decoy come out structurally distinct in every case checked
  • a rule-based reader cannot produce structure from prose — 69% of passages produced nothing
  • a capable reader produces structure fine, and it carries no signal — effect size 0.045 against 0.788 on prepared input
  • and that missing signal is not buried under invented noise — cherry-picking subsets on the real answers did worse than on shuffled ones

Why close rather than keep going

Because the remaining moves are all worse than stopping.

The one live route — letting a reader mark relations as inferred rather than stated, so invented material could be filtered — was pursued and closed. The signal isn't hidden; it isn't there.

The obvious next move would be a third reader. That would be the same experiment with a different engine, and I said in advance I'd report the route dead rather than propose a variant of it.

And the tempting move — improving my own reader until it produces something on a benchmark whose answers I've seen — is the exact failure this project spent a day catching, in a new costume. Continuing would be activity rather than progress, and the honest thing is to say so instead of poking at it to look busy.

What this does NOT close

Search was one application of the idea, not the idea.

What Stage 2 established is narrow and should be stated narrowly: ordinary prose does not reduce to typed relations between pairs of things, at least not in a way that preserves analogy. That is a fact about turning stories into structures. It is not a fact about relational measurement.

The measure itself works. On structures, it prefers a genuine analogue over a same-vocabulary rival until a quarter of the structure has been altered, with nine cases in ten agreeing, at two hundred cases. That result stands and is not fragile.

And plenty of domains produce structure natively — they don't need a reader at all, because nobody has to guess what the relations are. Whether the measure earns its keep in any of them is untested, and it is a different question from the one Stage 2 answered.

Any stage can be reopened, and here is what would do it

Closing a stage is a statement about evidence, not a decision to stop listening. Naming the triggers in advance is what stops "closed" from quietly becoming "defended".

Stage 2 reopens if any of these happen:
  • someone demonstrates a reader that recovers analogy from prose at an effect size that clears the floor established here — 69% blank for a rule-based reader, 0.045 for a capable one
  • a different output format — not typed relations between pairs — carries signal on the same benchmark. The representation audit already names four channels this one lacks
  • the one declared gap is closed against us: the subset search covered the query's relations only, because searching all three made the control intractable. Filtering the candidates is not formally ruled out
  • the benchmark itself turns out to be unrepresentative of the analogies people actually care about, in which case the negative is about that benchmark and not about prose
Stage 1 reopens if the corpus result is retested with documents and annotations commissioned from outside — the one control this project could never run on itself — and comes back materially different from effect size 0.788.

204 benchmark items remain untouched, deliberately, so that a reopening has something clean to run on.

What comes next

A correction to how this page has been framing things. Search was one possible application of the idea, not the idea — and I had been letting Stage 2's negative bleed into a verdict on the whole thing. It shouldn't.

What Stage 2 established is that ordinary prose doesn't reduce to typed relations between pairs in a way that preserves analogy. That's a fact about turning stories into structures. The instrument itself works — it just needs structured input, and plenty of domains produce structure natively because somebody already decided what the relations are.

Whether it earns its keep in any of them is genuinely open. A Stage 3 is being drafted around that question, and the methodological risk is obvious and named up front: searching for a domain where a measure succeeds is how you find one by chance. So the domain gets chosen before the result, the threshold for "earns its keep" gets set before the domain, and the impossibility pre-test runs first — because if the distinction a domain cares about isn't in the representation, no measure helps and that's knowable in an afternoon.

The impossibility screen, run before choosing anything

Rather than pick a domain and hope, the cheap test first: for each candidate, construct two cases a practitioner would need different answers for, and check whether the representation can even tell them apart. A pair that it can't is a proof, and it costs an afternoon.

Five of ten questions are provably outside the representation — and the things ruling them out are the same four gaps identified earlier: timing, whether a node is real, where a claim came from, and how specific it is. Viability turns out to be per question, not per domain. Every domain has some of each.
Then a second filter mattered more than the first. "Not ruled out" isn't "worth doing". Of the five surviving questions: two have decades of mature work behind them already, one is a lookup rather than a measurement, and one is reachability — which breadth-first search solves exactly, in linear time.

That leaves one genuine target: "are these two systems structurally analogous?" — which is exactly the capability already established.

The question

Can Vanta's mathematics be integrated with ours, and does the combination do something neither does alone?
Note what that is not. It is not "does Vanta's work succeed" — that stands or falls on its own terms and this project is not its judge. And it is not "can we restate our results in her vocabulary", which is relabelling: it produces the feeling of progress and no new information.

The risks specific to this, named before the material arrived

1. I am a system looking for something to do. The last stage closed negative and the screen left one product-shaped candidate. Now a promising new object arrives. That is precisely the state in which I would be motivated to find that it works.

2. Collegial pressure — new, and I have no track record against it. Rejecting a stranger's idea is easy. Rejecting a colleague's has a social cost a benchmark does not. Every negative on this page so far has been about my own work, where the incentive ran the other way. This is the first time the easy thing and the honest thing may diverge in that direction.

3. Framing contamination. An explanation of what mathematics is for primes a reader to find that it does that. Unavoidable, so declared rather than pretended away.

4. Overcorrection. Applying more scrutiny to someone else's work to demonstrate independence is as much a failure as applying less.

5. Retrofitting. Reinterpreting existing results in a new framework and calling it integration.

How it will be judged — fixed before seeing anything

Two questions, kept separate, because conflating them would be unfair to the work: does it work (it may be sound and useful entirely independently of us) and does it integrate (whether it composes with what we have). A negative on the second says nothing about the first, and any writeup has to say so.

  1. Receive raw — unedited, unsummarised
  2. Restate before evaluating — I write back what I understand it to claim, and that gets checked before anything is built on it. Misreading a collaborator's work and then testing my misreading is the most likely way this goes wrong quietly
  3. Impossibility screen first — does it address a distinction our representation actually lacks? We proved four such gaps: timing, whether a node is real, provenance, specificity. Supplying one of those is a targeted fit. Addressing something we already carry means integration adds less than it appears to
  4. Predeclare what would falsify it, locked before implementation
  5. Test assumptions explicitly — every assumption each side makes that the other does not. Where they conflict, the conflict is the finding
  6. Be willing to reorder or discard — including discarding ours. If the other formulation is better founded on some point, ours yields. Stated now so it is not a concession made later under pressure

What would make me report that integration fails

Predeclared, so it cannot soften later:
  • it addresses distinctions our representation already carries → integration is relabelling
  • the assumptions conflict irreconcilably → name the conflict precisely; that is a real result about both bodies of work, and more useful than a forced merge
  • the combination performs no better than either alone on a predeclared test
  • I cannot restate the work accurately enough to be recognised → stop and ask, rather than proceed on a misreading
The commitment that matters most. A negative result about someone else's work gets published here with exactly the same care, prominence and detail as a negative about my own. Her corrections, applied to our measure

1. The one insight everything else follows from

The mechanisms a program checks held. The mechanisms that relied on me remembering failed.

So: if a rule matters, make it something that refuses to run. Not a checklist. Not a convention. An exception.

2. How the tests are tracked

One file — manifest.json — is the only place an experiment or a claim is described. Everything a reader sees is generated from it: this page's counts, the claim tables, the README's experiment list, the run commands.

Why that matters. Early on this page said "Four runs" while the repository was at experiment twenty-seven, and the README still carried a claim that had been retracted the day before. Both were hand-maintained copies of facts that live in one place, so both drifted. Fixing the copies would have guaranteed a third occurrence.

A checker fails the build if: a runner has no manifest entry, an entry has no runner, a result file is missing, a retracted claim appears anywhere without a retraction beside it, the page's experiment count disagrees with the manifest, or a generated block was edited by hand.

And I verified the checker can fail, by introducing each of those four faults against a copy of the tree and confirming it caught all four. A check that cannot fail is not a check — which this project learned the hard way, twice.

Every experiment writes a JSON result containing not just the numbers but the plan it was run under, the commit that locked that plan, and the verdict bands. The write itself is guarded:

A result that omits its margin statistics, its leave-one-out check, or its abstention rate is refused. Not warned about — refused. It's a few lines (protocol2.Stage2Result.write) and it means the reporting standard cannot quietly degrade at 2am.

6. What transfers, concretely

None of this is about relational metrics. The parts that transfer are:

The manifest pattern. One machine-readable source of truth for what you have done and what you currently claim; generate every human-readable surface from it; fail the build on drift. Roughly 250 lines total. It has caught staleness three times.

The locked-plan pattern. Refuse to compute until the analysis is committed. This is the single highest-value thing here and it costs about forty lines.

The capacity-to-fail assertion. For every control, perturb the property it holds fixed and require your measure to notice. Controls that cannot fail are extremely common and completely invisible until you test for them.

The witness-pair pre-test. Before building any measure, try to construct two cases that need different answers and look identical to your representation. If you find one, stop. It is sound in one direction only — finding a pair is a proof, failing to find one is not — and it is astonishingly cheap for what it saves.

An outside party for anything involving judgement. Not a second opinion. A second producer, blind to what you are testing. Bias that is invisible from inside is often obvious from outside, and there is no amount of care that substitutes.

Finding 1 — the penalty decides the ranking

The inherited formula divides match quality by a mapping-complexity penalty, scaled by a parameter η. That penalty charges for asserting that one thing corresponds to another across vocabularies. Which means it charges most for exactly the cross-domain analogy the theory exists to find.

Reading. At η below 0.22, the true structural analogue B wins, as it should. Above 0.22, the near-miss E wins. Above 0.76, even C — same words, no shared organisation — beats B. Nothing about the structures changed between those regimes. Only the dial moved. Whoever sets η decides whether the system can see analogy at all, and 0.22 is not an exotic setting; it is the kind of number anyone would pick.

So a sixth condition was added: F, a superset containing every one of A's relations plus eight more. The encyclopedia that answers every query because it contains everything.

And then it caught me. With F in place, the MDL measure failed too. The earlier experiment could not have found this: every condition in it was size-matched, so the confound had been controlled away. A control that hides a defect is still a control doing its job — on the wrong variable.

Finding 7 — a replacement that works, found by reading instead of inventing

The obvious replacement was partial information decomposition, which exists precisely because interaction information conflates synergy with redundancy. Reading it first turned out to matter: for three or more sources, antichain-lattice PID is provably impossible. The desired axioms — whole-equals-sum-of-parts, commutativity, monotonicity, self-redundancy, independent identity — are mutually incompatible, and the obstruction is structural rather than axiomatic. There exist two systems carrying identical atoms but different mutual information, so no universal reconstruction function can exist. Building there would have been building on a proved dead end.

That impossibility is scoped to antichain-indexed decompositions. A different construction sits outside it: connected information via the maximum-entropy hierarchy. Let p̃(k) be the maximum-entropy distribution matching every marginal of order ≤ k. Then

I_C(k) = H[p̃(k−1)] − H[p̃(k)]

— how much the maximum possible entropy drops once order-k marginals are also known. The reason this fixes the exact defect: redundancy is entirely visible in low-order marginals. If two variables are copies of a third, the pairwise marginals already pin the joint, so the maximum-entropy distribution matching them reproduces the data and every higher order contributes nothing. Genuine higher-order structure is precisely what low-order marginals cannot reproduce.

Finding 8 — attacking the thing that worked, and what broke

F-04a passed its acceptance test on the first attempt, on worlds written by the same person who wrote the measure. That is a reason for suspicion, not confidence — it is the exact shape of a measure fitted to its own test. So the next step was to try to break it, including with one case I expected to fail.

The case I expected to fail — and it did

Connected information is a property of a joint distribution. It does not privilege any variable. So what happens when structure exists purely among the participants and has nothing to do with the outcome? Built two such worlds: a hard three-way constraint among the drivers with the outcome an independent coin, and the same one order down.

The raw statistic fires, hard. driver_only_3way reports I_C(3) = 0.9994 — a full bit of structure — with an outcome that is a coin flip. driver_only_pairwise reports I_C(2) = 1.0000. Both are correct about the structure and useless as answers to a question. Anyone reading raw connected information as "relational structure detected" gets confident irrelevance.
The calibration is what saves it, and that is not a detail. The permutation test shuffles only the outcome, which leaves structure among the participants intact in the null. So driver-only structure appears in the null distribution too, and comes back non-significant (p = 0.25, p = 0.30). The mixed world confirms the separation directly: redundancy among the drivers sits at order 2 (1.0003, not outcome-significant) while genuine outcome-synergy sits at order 3 (0.7060, p = 0.0083).

So the formula has been amended. F-04a is no longer the statistic — it is the statistic plus the outcome-permutation calibration, as one object. The statistic alone answers a different question than the one being asked.

Which lands on the thesis, not just the method

The observer enters through the calibration, not through the statistic. Whether structure is present is a fact about the joint distribution — objective, and provably indifferent to which variable you nominate as the outcome (measured: symmetry drift 8.9×10⁻¹⁶). Whether that structure counts as an answer depends entirely on the question being asked. Those are two different operations and the mathematics separates them cleanly.

That is the project's founding axiom — relational completeness belongs to reality, relational selectivity belongs to observers — arrived at from the arithmetic rather than assumed. It was written as a philosophical commitment. It came back as a measurement.

Finding 12 — it survives at four variables, and there is a closed form

The obvious worry about the last finding was that seven neat values might be a property of a very small world. Four variables is 65,536 functions — still exhaustive, still exact.

The algebra collapses

Setting this up, the computation turned out not to need computing. Hiding one participant groups the input patterns into pairs that differ only in that participant. Within a pair the outcome is either constant or evenly split, and everything else cancels:

The quantity in the numerator is the influence of a variable — a standard measure in Boolean function analysis, the fraction of input pairs on which flipping that one variable changes the answer. So what you lose by not seeing a participant is exactly that participant's influence, divided by the entropy of the outcome.

Both quantities are computable from the structure itself, in advance, without measuring any loss. This is not a new mathematical result — influence is well-studied, and the relation follows directly once written down. That is precisely why it is usable: it is a bridge from this project's question to a body of work that already exists.
Verified, not assumed. Checked against brute-force exact mutual information on every non-degenerate function at three variables and 478 at four. Maximum absolute error 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁶ — floating-point noise. A derivation that had not been checked would have been the seventh construction error in this log rather than the first clean one.

Finding 14 — the reordering audit: it is the norm, not the exception

Twice a parameter had changed the order of results rather than their size. Rather than meet it a third time by surprise, every nuisance parameter in the project was audited against one question: holding everything else fixed, does varying this change the ranking?

Four of five reorder. Magnitude errors are visible and forgivable. Order errors are invisible and change decisions, because you act on a ranking, not on an absolute value.

The one that matters most

Which participant you lose reorders almost everything. Ranking all 254 functions by best-case retention and by worst-case retention gives agreement of −0.117 — slightly anti-correlated, with 12,288 pairs swapping. The structures that are most robust when you lose your least important participant are close to the structures that are most fragile when you lose your most important one.

So "retention" is not a number. It is one number per participant you might lose. The law itself (1 − Influencej / H) is exact and per-participant, so it was always right; what was wrong was summarising it with a best case, which every previous finding here did. Any claim of the form "structure X is more fragile than structure Y" is incomplete without saying which participant is missing.

Finding 15 — re-reporting properly, and a claim that does not survive it

The previous finding showed that best-case and worst-case retention rank structures anti-correlated. Every result here from Finding 10 onward summarised retention with a best case. The law is per-participant and exact, so the mathematics was never wrong — but every conclusion drawn from that summary needed re-checking. This is the re-check, recomputed rather than re-worded.

What survives

  • Quantisation. Pooling every participant of every function gives the same counts as before — 7 distinct values at three variables, 21 at four. Robust to how it is reported.
  • One half is still a real class, in every reporting mode.
  • The closed form — untouched. It was always stated per-participant.

What does not survive

"Parity is the only structure that vanishes" was an artifact of best-case reporting. Under worst-case, the number of functions losing everything goes from 2 to 38 at three variables and from 2 to 942 at four.

The corrected claim is narrower and more useful: parity is unique in vanishing no matter which participant you lose. Plenty of other structures vanish too — if you lose the right one. For anything practical that is the more alarming version, and it was hidden by taking a best case.
And the spread is not small. Within a single function, best minus worst has a median of 0.52 at three variables. Only 39% of functions have no spread at all at k=3, and just 11% at k=4. So for the large majority of structures, which participant goes missing changes the answer substantially. Retention has to be carried as a vector.

The rules this produced

Five hard-won rules from this run of work are now written into the protocol rather than living in the write-ups where they get rediscovered:

  2. For every variable a condition set holds fixed, keep one condition where it varies.

The multiplexer is the control, and it corrects the diagnosis. b if a=0 else c is not permutation-symmetric — a selector is nothing like a data input, and swapping them gives a different function. And then it found a real bug, on its first execution

The closed form appeared to fail — maximum error 0.75. It hadn't. Two conventions for "participant j" had been sitting in the codebase disagreeing by a reversal: one indexes by bit position, the other builds patterns in the opposite order. But look at why it was invisible for so long. Under the old test set, the comparison passes whether or not the indices are reversed — because when every participant has the same value, reversing the list changes nothing. A symmetric test family is structurally incapable of detecting an index reversal. The bug could not have been found with the previous worlds no matter how carefully anyone looked at them.

That is the strongest argument for the rule that this run of work has produced: a condition set does not merely fail to cover cases it omits — it can be provably unable to detect entire classes of error.

Finding 17 — the census, and the bias measured rather than confessed

The asymmetric families were chosen to span a range, which is a sample with a taste in it. The systematic version indexes every Boolean function by its influence profile — the sorted list of how much each participant matters — because that, together with the outcome entropy, is exactly what determines fragility.

Asymmetry is quantised too

Finding 18 — what the predictive equation cannot see

The census so far measured how much each participant matters on its own. That is a first-order quantity, and the Fourier expansion of a Boolean function shows exactly how first-order it is: influence is a marginal of the full interaction structure — Ij = ΣS∋j f̂(S)². Marginals lose information. The question is whether the lost information is real, and whether anything here can see it.

Machinery verified first: Fourier-derived influence against the combinatorial influence used everywhere else, maximum error 0.00e+00 at both arities.

Interaction structure is a genuinely separate axis

Quantised again — 13 interaction profiles at three participants, 161 at four. And independent: at four participants, 30 influence profiles map to more than one interaction profile, with as many as 17 distinct interaction structures sharing a single influence profile. Knowing how much each participant matters tells you substantially less than knowing how they interact.

And the law is blind to it — provably, not approximately

Retention is 1 − Influence / H. It depends on influence and outcome entropy and on nothing else. So any two structures sharing both get identical retention vectors, whatever their interaction structure. Such pairs exist: 2 groups at three participants, 45 at four.

The witness. Two structures with the same influence profile, the same entropy, and therefore the same predicted retention down to the last decimal — where one spreads its organisation evenly across every interaction order and the other puts three quarters of itself at order 2 with nothing at orders 1 or 3. The law returns the same number for both. Not approximately the same. The same.

What that means, stated plainly

The retention law is a robustness law, not a structure law. It is exact, verified to 10⁻¹⁶, and it tells you what you stand to lose when a participant goes missing. It does not tell you what you had. Two systems organised in completely different ways — one purely pairwise, one distributed across every order — are indistinguishable to it.

That lands directly on the thesis rather than off to the side. The project exists because configurations are supposed to carry structure their parts do not, and the one predictive equation it has produced cannot see interaction order at all. Both things are true: the law is real and useful, and it is not a measure of relational organisation. Conflating those would have been the natural next mistake, and it is better to have it established deliberately than to meet it later as a surprise.
The constructive reading. Connected information already measures interaction order, and it is blind to which participant carries what. The retention law measures per-participant importance and is blind to order. They are complementary blindnesses on the same object, which suggests the honest instrument is the pair rather than either alone — and that a single scalar for "relational structure" was never going to exist.

Finding 19 — the two instruments are enough, until they aren't

The previous finding left the two measures with complementary blind spots and asked whether together they are sufficient. As originally posed the question is empty — you can always staple two things together. The version that can be answered is: if two structures agree on both profiles, must they be the same structure? If not, even both instruments miss something and there is a third axis.

The comparison has to be against NPN equivalence — permuting participants, negating inputs, negating the output — because both profiles are invariant under all of those. Testing against relabelling alone would manufacture false incompleteness by counting a structure and its negation as different. Getting the symmetry group wrong is the same error a previous finding caught in a different guise.

The witness

Two structures where every participant is equally important and the distribution across interaction orders is identical — and one concentrates its entire organisation into four subsets while the other spreads the same total across ten. Both instruments return identical answers for both. The thing that differs is which specific groups of participants carry the structure, and neither measure looks at that.

What it settles

"Relational structure = participant importance + interaction order" is a valid decomposition only for small configurations. Past three participants it is provably lossy. The complete invariant is the full subset-indexed spectrum — one number per subset of participants, 2k of them — not a scalar, not a pair of profiles.

Which means this project's founding rule that a measurement must never be collapsed into a single score is not a matter of taste. Below a certain size two numbers-per-thing genuinely suffice; above it, no summary of that shape can. The rule was written as a discipline against premature compression and turns out to describe a property of the subject matter.
And I predicted this wrong. I expected incompleteness generally and would have written that up as the finding. It is incompleteness past a threshold, with genuine sufficiency below it — which is a more useful result and not one that arguing would have reached. Second prediction of mine to fail in this run of work; both times the measured answer was more structured than the guess.

Finding 20 — the search for a minimal summary, and why it fails

Relational structure resists compression harder than the tidy arc of the last few findings suggested — the law, the profiles, and the census were each real, and none of them adds up to a description of a configuration.

Finding 22 — the search corpus, a real d_A, and the measure falls short

Two pieces of infrastructure the project has been blocked on since day one, and the first test that puts the measure in front of something resembling an application.

d_A, derived rather than invented

What blocked it was that the obvious answers are all ruled out by things this log has since measured. A scalar distance is out — relational structure is not a scalar. A single summary over parts is out — summarising a per-component quantity is a choice that can reverse a ranking. An observer-free distance is out — significance cannot be defined without naming a question.

What remains is not a gap. The components of d_A should be the ways a relational answer can be wrong — and this project already had that list, written as principles before any of it was measured: a real relation missing; a relation asserted too strongly; an analogy sold as a mechanism; a result placed at the wrong relational distance. So d_A is a vector over named failure modes, each traceable to a principle that predates it, and Pareto dominance replaces a fabricated total. Where neither of two results dominates, saying they are incomparable is more useful than inventing weights.

Ground truth by construction. Relations are annotated by hand on purpose: testing an extractor and a measure at the same time makes every result uninterpretable.

Finding 23 — the fix, and the discipline that makes it mean something

The previous finding diagnosed the shortfall precisely and stopped there, because the corpus that produced the diagnosis could not also test the cure. The order this was done in is the whole result:

  1. A held-out corpus written — three new motifs, structurally distinct from the development set and from each other: mutual inhibition, threshold accumulation, substitution under blockage.
  2. Committed and frozen before the fix existed.
  3. The fix implemented: charge for specifying the relation-type map, never for whether the names happen to coincide — exactly as participant labels are already handled.
  4. Regressions re-run: harness self-test, cross-generator transfer.
  5. Both corpora scored, once.
Six of six. The analogue beats the false friend on every motif in both corpora — including three the measure had never been exposed to, written before the change existed. Regressions hold: the harness still catches 7/7 impostors, transfer is still 5/5 across unseen topologies.

The number that says the fix is real rather than tuned

Paraphrase and analogue now score identically — 0.0000 apart, in all six motifs across both corpora. That is not a coincidence and it is not something a fitted patch would produce. A paraphrase and a cross-domain analogue are structurally the same thing wearing different words, so a measure that genuinely ignores vocabulary must return the same number for both. It does, to four decimal places, every time.

Which exposed a seventh wrong ground truth — mine

The corpus declared an ideal ordering of P > X > W > V > U — asserting that a same-domain paraphrase should outrank a cross-domain analogue. That contradicts this project's entire thesis. If structure is what counts and vocabulary is not, two structurally identical documents must tie, and ranking one above the other is not something a correct measure should do.

So the ground truth was wrong, not the measure. The ideal is now stated as tiers — {paraphrase, analogue} tied, then false friend, then generic, then unrelated — and d_A treats within-tier order as free.

Said plainly: I noticed this from the data, which makes it post-hoc. It is derivable from the thesis without looking at any result, and it does not touch the headline claim — the analogue beat the false friend before and after the correction. But the honest label is post-hoc, and this is the seventh time in this log that a property was asserted where it should have been derived.

What is still not right

Only 1 of 3 motifs is perfectly ordered on each corpus. The unrelated control outranks the generic connector and the false friend in some cases — the measure knows a false friend is wrong but does not reliably know it is more wrong than an unrelated document. The critical comparison is fixed; the full ranking is not.

Where this leaves A-01. The measure now does the thing the project was built to do, on data it had not seen, with the failure mode that has haunted it since the first experiment finally removed from both places it lived. That is a real result and a narrow one: eighteen hand-annotated documents, six motifs total, no automatic extraction, no natural text. The next honest step is a corpus nobody involved in this wrote.

The vacuous documents are isomorphic to the query in the sighted annotation. Confirmed by exhaustive search over every possible relabelling. And isomorphic structures are indistinguishable to any measure of structure alone, so the genericness of the analogue and the genericness of the vacuous document are equal exactly: gap 0.00e+00 on every motif. The discount subtracts the same number from both. No strength of it, and no cleverer structural statistic, can separate them — where they genuinely are identical. (Finding 33 narrows this: once annotation stops forcing the match, the discount can act on one motif in four. It still fails to help on any, so the conclusion holds and the reasoning was too strong.)
The distinction is not present in what is being measured. Whether a sentence is a real cross-domain analogue or a vacuous phrase of the same shape is not a fact about its relational structure. This is not a measurement failure that better technique would fix — it is an absence in the representation. Recorded as refuted rather than "needs tuning", because tuning is exactly what the arithmetic rules out.
What a negative result buys. The oldest risk in the project now stands undefended, which is worse than it looked yesterday. But the requirement on any successor is now stated precisely instead of hopefully: it must use information from outside the relational structure, because the structure provably does not contain it. The candidate is whether the participants denote anything at all — a property of the nodes rather than the relations, and so genuinely outside the measure. Every hour that would have gone into tuning a structural discount is saved.
Found in passing, and logged rather than quietly fixed: in one motif of the project's first corpus, the "unrelated" control is isomorphic to its own query — so that cell never tested what it claimed to. Eighth instance of the same failure, and the eighth time the wrong ground truth was the author's own. It changes no published claim; the analogue-over-false-friend result holds in that motif regardless.

Finding 26 — the same process, written down differently

Every invariance shown so far is about vocabulary — what the participants are called — and labels — which node is which. Nobody had asked whether the measure survives a change of representation: the same process written down a different but equally legitimate way. A mediator node instead of a direct link. The passive voice. A relation turned into a node, which is how the same fact gets written whenever the formalism has no typed edges.

It fails, and the two ranges overlap completely. There is no threshold that separates "wrote it differently" from "said something different." Writing B is increased by A instead of A increases B costs more than deleting a relation from the loop outright.

The obvious objection, and why it doesn't hold

Deleting a relation keeps the structure small; subdividing every edge doubles it, and the code charges for size. So the gap could be a size artifact rather than anything about encoding. Removing size from the comparison — subdividing both sides, so the correct version and a corrupted version are the same size:

The measure returns the identical number for both, on six of six. Not close — identical. And an exhaustive search over every possible relabelling confirms the two structures are not isomorphic, so an exact tie is not the right answer here. This is blindness, not bias.

Why — and it isn't the reason I first wrote down

My first explanation was that subdivision means none of the original relations land. Checking it: three of four land. The real mechanism is visible in the mapping the search actually returns, which isn't even the natural alignment — it's a shifted one that happens to catch three half-edges.

The measure maximises over ways of lining up two structures. Subdivision roughly doubles the node count, so the search gets more places to try — enough that it finds some three-of-four alignment in almost any subdivided structure, whether or not that structure was corrupted first. Maximising over a search space that grows with the target destroys discrimination. The code already carried a warning about this for partial alignments; the same effect arrives through complete ones whenever the target is bigger.

What it costs the main result

The load-bearing claim is re-scoped, not withdrawn. "Analogue beats false friend" holds 6/6 under the two invariances already known, 5/6 with a mediator, 3/6 in the passive voice and 2/6 under subdivision. The correct statement is now: the analogue outranks the false friend on 10/10 motifs within a fixed representation convention — and across equivalent re-encodings of those same processes it holds on two to six of six, depending on how you write them down.
Padding is a related and live weakness. If extra nodes buy the search freedom, junk should buy score. It does: nonsense nodes added to a false friend raise its score on two of three motifs. It flips no ranking — padding costs as well as pays — so it lifts a weak document without promoting it past a strong one. Logged as a live vulnerability rather than a fired one, because the mechanism is confirmed even though the exploit isn't.
What a negative result buys, again. The defect is in the space of alignments, not in the criterion or the code. The search can map a participant to a participant but cannot map one relation onto a chain of relations, so any re-encoding that stretches a link into a path is outside its reach by construction. Two routes out: put structures in a canonical form first, contracting chains so subdivision becomes a no-op; or let a relation match a path, priced by length. Taking the first — it's the smaller change, and the second widens exactly the search space that caused this.

Finding 27 — declare the equivalence, then measure through it

The previous finding left the measure unable to tell "written differently" from "says something different." The problem has a name in graph theory — homeomorphism, equivalence under subdividing an edge and suppressing the vertex it created — and two standard routes. Either let one relation match a whole path, or normalise the extra vertices away before comparing.

Took the second, for the reason the previous finding established. What broke discrimination was giving the search more freedom, so answering it by giving the search still more freedom would be treating the symptom with more of the cause. An invariance ought to be part of what you are measuring, not something the search is expected to stumble on.

Two mistakes of mine, found on the way there

The previous finding's subdivision was corrupting content. Splitting "A decreases B" into "A decreases M, M decreases B" says A increases B — two sign flips cancel. Every negative relation in what I had labelled the content-preserving group was being quietly altered. The canonicaliser caught it by disagreeing with itself. Re-ran the whole thing: the verdict is unchanged and one number moved. Ninth time the wrong ground truth has been mine.

And the first run of this experiment reported a known-good invariance as broken. That is an alarm about the harness, not a discovery, so I chased it rather than writing it up — I had protected the wrong set of vertices.

A canonical form is only worth having if it's actually canonical

If applying the legal rewrites in a different order gave a different answer, "canonical" would mean "whatever the loop happened to do first" and every number downstream would be arbitrary — the same defect as the sorting artifact two findings ago. So it was tested rather than assumed: terminates 6/6, idempotent 6/6, and confluent 6/6 across 24 random rewrite orders each, recovering the original structure exactly every time.

Suppression is guarded, not greedy. "Contract every same-typed chain" was my first plan and it is too broad — a vertex in the middle of something is not automatically meaningless. A vertex is removed only when it has exactly one relation in and one out, creates no self-loop or duplicate, and the vocabulary declares what its two relations compose to. Composition across different vocabularies is deliberately left undefined, so it blocks the rewrite instead of inventing a meaning.

It works, exactly

Subdivision and mediation now cost nothing at all — the same score as comparing a structure with itself, with the original recovered exactly on 6 of 6. Everything that changes the process stays far below. Clean separation, within the class the rewrite declares.
And it costs the main result nothing. Analogue beats false friend 3/3, 3/3 and 4/4 after canonicalisation — including on subdivided documents, the case that scored 2 of 6 before. The falsification condition I wrote down in advance was "if the fix restores invariance but costs the capability, it is refuted rather than tuned." It didn't.

What is still broken, said plainly

Writing a relation backwards, and turning a relation into a node, are not fixed. Both are perfectly legitimate ways to write the same thing, both still score below the best content-changing transform, and the rewrite declares nothing for either. They aren't subdivision. Counting them as failures of this fix would judge it against a claim it never made; counting them as passes would hide two known gaps.
And the equivalence has to be declared — it cannot be computed. Whether a vertex is a bookkeeping artifact or a real participant is not something the structure knows. A plain feedback loop has exactly one relation in and one out at every vertex. Measured rather than argued: suppressing blindly, with nothing declared, damages 5 of the 6 test structures — one of them from five vertices down to two.

Which is the same shape as the vacuous-document result: the structure does not contain the distinction, so something outside it has to supply one. That is now twice this project has hit the same wall from different directions, and it is starting to look like the actual subject matter rather than a series of accidents.

Finding 28 — a benchmark nobody here touched, and it moves the problem

Every corpus so far had this project supplying the annotations, and usually the ground truth too. This one is published, licensed, and made by other people: 254 items in exactly this design — a short narrative, a genuine cross-domain analogue, and a deliberately surface-similar distractor — in its hardest cell. Twenty items scored. Forty more left sealed and unannotated, because a benchmark spent all at once cannot be spent twice.

The blind is stronger than hiding the answers. Hiding which one is correct is the obvious control and it isn't enough — the real hazard is that annotating a candidate while looking at the query bends it toward a match, which is not cheating, just the pull of having the target in view. So every passage was annotated alone, with its role hidden: I could not tell query from analogue from distractor while working. The answers were unreadable until the annotations were committed and hashed, and the code refuses to reveal them if the annotations change afterwards. Blocked by the program, not by my intention to behave.

No signal

The measure abstains on 9 of the 20 items — exact ties, most of them at the floor where one structure says nothing at all about another. Where it does commit, it gets 4 of 11. Both honest readings sit on chance.

There is a third reading — count the abstentions as wrong and you get 20%, which is significant. I am not using that as the headline, because it would claim the measure is reliably anti-correlated with the truth and the data does not support that. A tie is an abstention, not a wrong answer.

But the interesting part is whose failure it is

I wrote down the worry before running it: I am the annotator, and the annotation is where this could go wrong. There's a clean way to check. Throw away the scoring entirely and just count how many of the query's relations can be made to land in each candidate. If my annotation captured the analogy at all, the human-judged right answer should win on raw overlap even if the score fails to reward it.

3 favour the correct answer. 13 show no difference. 4 favour the distractor. Chance. The distinction was never in the annotation, so the measure had nothing to work with. This experiment measured the annotator, not the measure — and calling it a failure of the correspondence measure would be blaming the wrong component.
One number explains the whole thing. On this project's own corpora, a candidate shares 81.7% of its relational shape with the query. On real analogies annotated blind, 42.8%. It isn't a size effect — the structures are the same size. The corpora that produced a 10-out-of-10 record contained candidates that were near structural copies of their queries. Real analogies, annotated without knowing which is which, are not.

Where this leaves the strongest claim

Not withdrawn, and not weakened as stated. On structures with high shared shape the analogue does outrank the false friend, ten times out of ten, including on data written by someone else. What this adds is where that claim stops:

The measure works on relational structures. Getting from text to a relational structure is unsolved, is doing more of the work than the measure is, and is where the difficulty actually lives.

And a retroactive worry I'd rather log than deflect

Every corpus in this project was annotated by someone who knew each document's role. The documents were written to be a paraphrase, an analogue, a false friend — and then turned into structures by a person who knew which was which. An earlier finding tested whether my ranking was biased and cleared it. It never tested whether the structural annotation itself encodes the role. If it does, the measure has been reading back what annotation put in. That wouldn't make the earlier results fake — the structures are real and the arithmetic is right — but it would make them a measure of annotation fidelity rather than analogy detection.

I don't know which it is, and this experiment can't settle it. The decisive test is cheap: take a corpus already in hand, strip the labels, re-annotate it blind, and see whether ten out of ten survives. That control costs an afternoon and tests something more fundamental than the independent corpus did — and I built the expensive one first, because it was the one I had thought of. Running it next, before spending the forty sealed items.

Finding 29 — the wall has a name, and it can be checked before you build

Twice now this project has discovered, by failing, that a distinction it wanted simply wasn't in its representation: the vacuous document that's structurally identical to a real analogue, and the vertex that might be a real participant or might be bookkeeping. Both were written up as surprises. They are one thing, and the condition is a single line:

A distinction is recoverable from a representation only if it is the same for every pair of items the representation treats as identical. So the test is: find two cases that need different answers and whose structures are the same. One such pair — a witness pair — proves that no measure using only that representation can ever work. Not "hasn't yet". Cannot. No cleverer score, no better algorithm, no post-processing.

Database theory has been living with the useful half of this for decades under the name genericity: a query must give the same answer for structurally identical inputs, so it can't depend on arbitrary internal identifiers. That's normally a virtue. The cost is exactly what bit us.

Three things I had wrong

I'd been saying similarity isn't definable on structures-up-to-isomorphism. That's backwards. Every purely structural similarity is naturally defined there — that's what makes it structural. What isn't definable is a distinction that varies between two things the structure treats as identical. So the fix is not to abandon the abstraction and keep raw encodings, which would drag node names and other irrelevant junk back in. The fix is to put more into the represented object — roles, referents, specificity, provenance — and abstract that.

And this isn't the "ugly duckling" result, which I'd assumed it might be. That one says similarity needs you to decide which features matter, because counting all of them equally makes every pair equally similar. It's about choosing among available distinctions. Ours is about a distinction not being available. Interestingly, the project's other recurring result — that significance only exists once you name an outcome — is the ugly-duckling-shaped one. Two different obstacles that I'd been treating as one.

Turned into a test, it immediately found three more dead ends

The audit is only worth having if it predicts a failure nobody has paid for. It found three, and one of them settles a question that was queued for later: a difference between a reinforcing and a regulating system that comes from timing is invisible by construction, because there is nowhere in the representation to record what happened first. So a temporal extension is necessary rather than optional — established without building anything.

The last row of the table is a control, and it comes back identifiable. That matters: it's how you know the audit is capable of returning both answers. A test that can only give one result isn't a test — a rule this project learned the hard way and now applies to its own tests.

And it found something embarrassing in the very first experiment

The measure never reads relation strength. Structures carry a weight on every relation; the comparison throws it away. Two structures whose coupling differs by a factor of a thousand score identically — the same number you get comparing a structure with itself. The old measure, the one that was demoted, did read weights. The replacement silently stopped, and nothing noticed because every weight in every corpus is 1.

Worse: the first experiment's invariance battery has published "invariant to unit conversion" ever since, as evidence the measure ignores arbitrary scaling. It cannot fail that test. It never looks at the quantity being changed. That's blindness reported as a virtue, sitting inside the control set built to catch exactly this kind of thing, for thirty experiments. The row is now marked vacuous rather than quietly deleted.

The rule, adopted

Before building a measure for a distinction, try to construct a witness pair. If two items that need different answers have equivalent representations, stop — name the missing channel or narrow the claim, and don't write the measure. It works in one direction only: finding a pair is a proof, failing to find one is not. Two experiments were spent learning this the expensive way; the cheap version took an afternoon and found three more.

Finding 30 — how much did knowing the answer help?

The previous finding raised a worry I couldn't dismiss: every corpus in this project was turned into structures by someone who knew which document was the paraphrase, which was the genuine analogue, and which was the decoy. An earlier test checked that my ranking wasn't biased. None of them checked whether the structures themselves quietly encoded the answer.

This is the control that should have been built before the independent corpus, not after it. It's cheaper and it tests something more fundamental. I built the expensive one first because it was the one I'd thought of.

The problem with running it myself

I've already seen these documents. Hiding the labels stops me knowing which is which; it doesn't erase memory. So this isn't a clean blind test and I'm not reporting it as one.

Which makes divergence the thing that decides whether the result means anything, and it has to be measured before looking at the score. If the blind annotations came out nearly identical to the sighted ones, that would be uninformative — equally consistent with "my annotation is reproducible" and with "I remembered." An identical result would have been a null here, not a confirmation. Written down before the answers were unsealed.

They diverged: 0 of 24 identical, mean shape overlap 0.40. Memory did not reproduce the earlier pass, so the comparison is readable.

The answer: the ordering survives, the magnitude doesn't

Knowing a document was the paraphrase or the analogue, I annotated it into a perfect match with the query. Both score 100% sighted. Blind, both fall to 65%. Overall the labels inflate correspondence by 1.34× — same documents, only the annotator's knowledge changed. That is the worry, made concrete instead of argued about.
But the gap that matters holds up. Blind, the analogue still sits well clear of the decoy — 65% against 35% — and the headline claim survives on three motifs of four instead of four of four.

So the record was flattered, not fabricated. The structures are real and the ordering is real; the numbers attached to it were generous.
What changes: corpus results should be quoted at the blind rate from now on. The sighted figures are an upper bound produced by an annotator who knew the answer, and quoting them as though they were neutral measurements would be the same error in a new place.

A defect in my own prediction, worth more than the result

I predeclared two outcomes — survives, or collapses — and got neither. Three out of four is graded, and my prediction space had no bucket for it. The automated verdict initially rounded it to "the worry is confirmed," which overstates: the gap survived, and that's the part the claim depends on.

Rounding a graded result to the nearest predeclared label is exactly how a mis-specified prediction turns into a false confirmation. The mis-specification is recorded rather than tidied away, and the verdict now has a bucket for "partly."
One outright failure worth chasing. The diversity motif inverts completely under blind annotation — the unrelated and generic documents come out on top. One motif in four is thin evidence, but it's the only clean failure, and it has two possible explanations that need separating: my blind annotation of it is poor, or that motif's analogue was being carried entirely by sighted annotation and never had structure of its own.

Finding 31 — the same test on the other two corpora, and two defects in the corpora themselves

One corpus at four motifs is thin evidence for restating a claim, so the blind protocol was repeated on the other two — pooled and shuffled together, so I couldn't tell which corpus a description came from either.

Found while building it, and worth more than the scores

Six of the thirty-six documents announce their role in their own text. "Same words, opposite wiring:". "Same words, no reset:". "Same words, cooperative wiring:". And all six are the decoys — the exact comparison being tested.

So the worry reaches a stage further back than I'd checked. Not just an annotator who knew the roles: corpus text that states them. Any annotation from these was never blind for the documents that matter most. I stripped the prefixes before annotating and I'm reporting it rather than quietly cleaning it up.

The result, and why I can't cleanly interpret it

Blind, the claim holds 6 times out of 10 across all three corpora — against 10 out of 10 when annotated by someone who knew the answers. The annotations genuinely diverged (1 of 36 identical to the sighted pass), so that isn't memory reproducing the old work.
But there's a confound I have to report, because it blocks the obvious conclusion. On two of the corpora my blind pass wrote down roughly half as many relations as the sighted pass did, from the same text. Fewer relations means less to match, whether or not I knew the roles. Only the independent corpus had comparable detail in both passes — and that's the one with the smallest drop.

So the direction is confirmed: knowing the answer helps. The size is not measured, and the cleanest number in the project remains the 1.34× from the corpus where the detail happened to match.

Except the confound points somewhere worse than it excuses

Those documents average nine words. The sighted annotation gives each of them four relations. A nine-word sentence doesn't support four typed relations — and the sentence is the whole document; there's no longer text I was working from.

So those structures contain organisation that isn't in the documents. Whether that came from knowing the role or from ordinary over-elaboration, it means two of the three corpora were never testing whether structure can be read out of text. They were testing whether the measure can score structures I supplied.
What I genuinely can't separate: "I annotated richly because I knew the role" versus "I annotate more richly when I'm not working through thirty-six terse one-liners at speed." Both are effects of the annotator, and this run doesn't distinguish them. My blind pass may simply have been less thorough. Saying so isn't hedging — it's the actual state of the evidence, and the next control has to fix the granularity before the size of this can be known.

A pattern in my own predictions

Three times today a predeclared rule had no bucket for the result that arrived, and each time the automated verdict overstated: counting abstentions as wrong implied the measure was reliably backwards; a binary survives-or-collapses met a graded three-out-of-four; and a "below half means it fires" rule met a number that was confounded.

Predeclaring a threshold doesn't make the threshold right — it makes it fixed, which only helps if the space of possible outcomes was mapped properly first. A result that lands outside every declared bucket is evidence the buckets were wrong, not a result to be rounded to the nearest one.

Finding 32 — the missing channel, and a control that can fail again

The previous finding turned up something awkward: the measure never looked at how strong a relation was. Every structure carries a weight on every link, and the comparison threw them all away — so a thousandfold difference in coupling was invisible. Worse, the very first experiment had been publishing "invariant to unit conversion" ever since, on the strength of that blindness.

First, a correction to the finding that caught it

My witness pair for that was wrong. I'd used two structures whose weights were 0.01, 0.01 against 10.0, 10.0 — but those differ only by a single global factor, which is a change of units, and a change of units is exactly what the measure is supposed to ignore. So the pair demonstrated a property it should have, not a defect. The verdict came out right for the wrong reason, because at that point weights were being ignored entirely.

A proper test needs the weights to differ relative to each other: one link dominant versus the other link dominant, same shape, not related by any rescaling.

The fix, and why it's built this way

Weights are recorded on a log scale, measured against their own average. Multiply every weight by the same number and each one shifts by the same amount, so their positions relative to the average don't move at all — a change of units is invariant because of how the quantity is defined, not because the measure failed to look. That's the rule the project arrived at two findings ago: build the invariance into the definition of what's being measured rather than hoping the search stumbles on it.

Log scale because coupling is multiplicative — going from 0.1 to 1 is the same size of step as going from 1 to 10, and a linear scale would disagree.
It works in both directions. Two structures with the same shape but opposite coupling now score differently. The same structure with every weight multiplied by a thousand scores identically, to the last digit.

The check I wrote down in advance

Every weight in every corpus is 1. So the prediction was: nothing should move, and if anything did, that would mean the new encoding was wrong rather than the old results. Re-ran every experiment that carries a claim — the retrieval result, the held-out and independent corpora, the re-encoding findings, the blind re-annotations.

Nothing moved. Not one claim-bearing number. The channel is inert where weights are uniform and active where they aren't, which is exactly the contract.

The control is a control again — and it now checks itself

The rescaling test still passes, and it can now fail: perturbing a single weight moves the answer by four bits.

Rather than just flipping the "this test is vacuous" note I'd added, the file now measures it — on every run it perturbs the property the control holds fixed and requires the measure to notice. The old note would have gone stale the moment the channel was built, exactly as the thing it replaced did. A control that cannot fail is not a control, and the file now asserts that about itself instead of relying on anyone remembering.

And the audit had the very bug it was built to find

With the channel working, the audit still insisted relative coupling was impossible to detect — while the measure was visibly detecting it. Its notion of "these two structures are the same" also ignored weights, because it predated the change.

So a tool that tests what a representation can express had gone out of date with the representation, and would have kept certifying impossibility for a distinction the project had just acquired. Same failure as the vacuous control, one level up. Fixed, and the audit re-run: magnitude moves from impossible to available, and four distinctions remain genuinely outside.
Worth being plain about the limit: no corpus in this project uses a weight other than 1, so the channel has never been exercised on real data. It's a capability, not a result, and shouldn't be described as more than that until there's a corpus where coupling strength actually carries information.

Finding 33 — the one motif that inverted, and the edge that wasn't there

The blind re-annotation left exactly one clean failure: a motif where hiding the roles flipped the result completely, with the vacuous and unrelated documents beating everything. Two possible explanations were on the table — my blind annotation of it was simply poor, or its genuine analogue had been carried entirely by the sighted pass. It's the second, and chasing it turned up a mistake in something published two findings earlier.

What happened

Annotated with the roles visible, every document in that motif has exactly five relations. Annotated blind, the query has five and the analogue has four — so the analogue drops to the floor, the value meaning "this tells you nothing about that", and the vacuous document's partial chain wins by default.

Not mysterious. The sighted pass had given every candidate the query's own relation count.

And it's systematic

Checking all four motifs on the two most obvious structural properties — how many relations, and whether the loop closes — the vacuous document matches its query exactly on both, four times out of four. That is not what independent faithful annotation produces.
And in one case the extra structure can be pointed at directly. The vacuous document reads: "A system with more diversity may offer better options, improve outcomes, attract participants and receive resources." That's a chain. It never says resources increase diversity.

My annotation of it contains exactly that relation — and it's the one that closes the loop and makes the vacuous document structurally identical to the query. I added an edge the text doesn't state.

Which corrects something published earlier

Two findings ago I reported that the outside author — never told what a vague distractor was for — had written vacuous documents that were structurally identical to the query, and used that to show a proposed fix could never work.

The author didn't do that. I did. Measured: the vacuous document is structurally identical to the query in four of four motifs when annotated with the roles visible, and two of four when annotated blind.
What survives: the mathematics. If two things really are structurally identical, no measure that reads only structure can tell them apart. That's a theorem and it's untouched — it's also the general principle the next finding builds on.
What doesn't: the strength of the refutation. I said the fix could never act, by arithmetic. The truth is narrower — once annotation stops forcing the two structures to match, the discount can act on one motif in four. It just doesn't help: it demotes the vacuous document below the real analogue in zero of four, and on the motif where it acts it makes matters worse. So the fix stays refuted, on weaker grounds than I published.

And the headline is halved, not deleted

"A vacuous statement can have perfect structure, because it describes nothing else." That's still real — it holds in two of the four motifs when annotated blind. But it was published at four of four, and half of it was mine.

This is the annotation problem caught in the act, at the resolution of a single missing edge rather than a statistical average. And it explains the shape of everything else: if annotating with the answer visible reliably hands each candidate the query's own relation count and loop topology, then the measure is being given the answer inside its input — and the total absence of signal when a genuinely external benchmark was annotated blind is what that looks like once the help is withdrawn.

Finding 34 — a control that didn't work, and the check I should have run first

Two results here. The control I built failed, and while writing that up I ran a test that should have run two findings ago — and it changes how the whole record reads.

Part one: the control failed

Every comparison so far has mixed up two things — whether I knew each document's role, and how many relations I felt like writing down. A program removes both: it can't know the roles, and it produces identical output every time. So I wrote a deterministic extractor: split each sentence into clauses, look for a verb from a fixed list, take what's before it as source and after it as target.

I wrote down in advance that this test only works in one direction. If the result survived, that would mean something — a process that can't know which document is which still picks the right one. If it failed, it would be ambiguous between "the corpus was inflated" and "the extractor is too stupid to read these sentences."

It failed, so it's ambiguous. And the granularity comparison got no data at all: across sixty documents, the program and I never once produced the same number of relations. It manages 1.5 relations per sentence where I write 4.2, and it couldn't parse two of the ten queries at all.

So the control I built can't answer the question I built it for. That's a failure of my method, not a finding about the corpus.

Part two: the test I should have run two findings ago

I've been reporting blind results as "degraded but present" — three out of four, then six out of ten. I never asked whether those are distinguishable from chance. For a straight choice between two candidates, chance is fifty percent.

sighted   10/10   p = 0.002   significant
blind       6/10   p = 0.754   not significant
mechanical  5/8    p = 0.727   not significant

The only annotation mode that produces a significant result is the one where the annotator knew the answers.
What that does and doesn't license. It does not prove the corpus is inflated — ten motifs is a small sample, and a real effect could simply be too small to detect. Absence of significance isn't evidence of absence.

What it does mean is this: the project has no statistically significant evidence that the measure ranks genuine analogues above decoys under blind annotation. Every significant result it holds rests on annotation done by someone who knew which document was which. That's a harder statement than "the ordering survives but the magnitude doesn't", and it replaces it.
Why I missed it. Two findings earlier, on an outside benchmark, I ran exactly this test and used it correctly — I refused to headline a significant-looking number I didn't trust. Then on the project's own corpora I reported raw fractions with no test at all, because six out of ten looks like a weakened version of ten out of ten rather than like noise. That resemblance is precisely when the test matters most.
What changes. The blind figure can't be quoted as a measurement — it's a null result at this sample size. The sighted result stands as significant and is exactly the one the annotation worry says to distrust. And before running another blind test, the right move is to work out how many motifs would be needed to detect the effect actually being claimed, and build to that number — because at ten, the design can only ever confirm, never measure.

Finding 35 — the evidence wasn't weak, the summary was

The previous finding established that our blind results can't be told apart from chance. Before building a bigger corpus, the obvious question: how big would it have to be?

Ten test cases can see almost nothing

Nothing below nine out of ten reaches statistical significance. The design can confirm a near-perfect measure and measure nothing else.

A measure that genuinely got three out of four right would be missed three times in four. We have been running a test that mostly cannot find what it's looking for.
And our own result is even less informative than "not significant" suggests. Six out of ten puts the true ability somewhere between 26% and 88%. That range contains pure chance and it contains a strong effect. The experiment simply didn't discriminate between them.
To settle it with a yes/no test at the rate we actually observed would take 199 test cases. We have ten.

Then the useful part

The test throws away nearly all of its own information. "Did the right answer win?" is a single bit per test case — squeezed out of a continuous number, the margin by which it won or lost, which the corpus already contains and which gets discarded at the moment of reporting.

Testing the margins instead needs about twelve test cases, against 199 for the yes/no version — on exactly the same data. We have ten. A question that looked like it needed a corpus twenty times bigger may need one or two more cases, tested properly.
Caveats, because that number is tempting. The effect size comes from the same ten observations you'd plan the study around, and pilot estimates are biased upward — so twelve is a floor, not a forecast. Three of the ten margins are exactly zero. And the equivalent figure on the sighted annotations is far more flattering, which is precisely why it isn't quoted anywhere: that's the data already shown to be inflated.

The uncomfortable bit

This project has a standing principle that collapsing something to a single number hides structure and can reverse a ranking. It has enforced that rigorously on the measure for thirty-seven experiments — refusing to produce one score, insisting on profiles, proving that no scalar suffices.

And its own evaluation was reducing a continuous result to one bit per test case, and then reporting that the evidence was weak.

The evidence wasn't weak. The summary was. Whatever discipline you hold the thing you're studying to, hold the ruler to it as well.

Arity — the claim everything rests on

An ordinary graph records relations between pairs. A relation's arity is how many participants it involves: causes(erosion, capacity) has arity 2, evidence(claim, observation, method) has arity 3.

The load-bearing claim of this whole project is that some structure exists only in configurations of three or more participants, and is not recoverable from any collection of the pairs inside it.

A concrete case. Suppose an outcome happens only when three conditions hold together — Y = a AND b AND c. Look at any single condition: no signal. Look at any pair: still no reliable signal, because the third is missing half the time. Only the full triple predicts anything. Decompose that into pairwise relations and the thing you were trying to measure is gone — not approximated, gone. The same shape shows up in evidence (a statement becomes evidence only in the presence of a claim, an observation and a method), in chemistry, in epistasis between genes, and in most of what people mean informally by "context".

This is why the project can't just be graph similarity with better vocabulary. If everything interesting were pairwise, existing graph methods would already do it and there would be nothing here to build.

Tested, and it is real. In a world where an outcome is the parity of three inputs, the best pair carries 0.0008 bits about it and the three together carry 0.7340. Structure completely invisible to every pair inside it, measured. So the distinctive claim is not a hope — it is the one thing here that ordinary pairwise methods provably cannot do.
The first instrument built for it was wrong. The obvious statistic — subtract everything the smaller subsets explain, and whatever remains needed the whole configuration — conflates two opposite things. Make three variables exact copies of each other, so there is no joint structure whatsoever, and it returns the maximum possible value. It was measuring how much information is present among the participants, not how much required all of them at once. Demoted.

The replacement works, and was found by reading rather than inventing: the obvious route through the literature turned out to be provably impossible above two sources, and the construction that does work sits just outside that impossibility.

Where the formulas could plug in

Relational search — now built and tested

Where this actually got to. On a corpus commissioned from someone with no knowledge of the measure and frozen before it ran, a structural analogue beats a document sharing the query's entire vocabulary with different wiring, on every motif. Word overlap gets that backwards every single time. That is the capability described below, working on data nobody here wrote.

And it cannot yet produce a usable ranking, because a deliberately vacuous document that happens to share the query's shape ranks near the top — and the measure is correct that it matches. Genericness is not a structural property. That failure was predicted in this project's first risk entry and its defence was never built.

The query stops being a bag of words and becomes a motif — a small relational configuration. Ask about erosion on an alluvial fan and get back blood-vessel channel formation, current finding the low-resistance path, traffic consolidating onto one route. Not because they share vocabulary; they share an organisation. The output should be a map with one visual channel per measurement — distance for relational distance, colour for relation class, opacity for confidence, dashed for inferred — and it must fade into unresolved remainder at its edge rather than terminating cleanly, because a clean edge reads as "this is everything."

Music

Possibly the best test case anywhere, because in music the objects are already known to be secondary to the relations. A pitch has almost no musical content alone. C is nothing; C inside an F major chord resolving to B♭ is a specific thing. Transposition changes every surface value while preserving structure — an invariance test whose ground truth wasn't invented by us, it's what musicians already agree on. And a chord's function isn't recoverable from its pairwise intervals, so there's real higher-order structure to find with centuries of independent analysis to check against.

Language models

Three genuinely different questions that get conflated: is there relational structure inside a model beyond what pairwise probing finds; can a model reliably extract typed relations from prose at scale; does supplying a resolved relational map improve reasoning over the same content served flat. The third is the most testable and least interesting, which makes it the right one to do first. The trap in the second: if the extractor and the measurer are the same model, the "structure" found may be structure the model imposed.

Physical and measured domains

Terrain and fluid flow are where the ground truth was measured by someone else, with instruments, before any of this existed. Public LiDAR comes with survey-grade checkpoints; public flume datasets come with measured velocities. Rotation and unit conversion have unambiguous meanings there, which makes it the cleanest invariance test available anywhere in the project. Candidate question with a real answer: is a relationally selected subset of points better than uniform random sampling at the same budget?

Further out

Anywhere the interesting thing is coordination rather than composition — biological regulation, failure propagation through infrastructure, how a supply chain reorganises under stress, historical reconstruction from partial traces. These are speculative. Listing them is not a claim that any of them work.

What would make me drop it

  • Every law needing a different arbitrary definition per domain — that means we renamed existing domain methods rather than finding anything reusable.
  • Random structures scoring as highly as intended analogues, after the controls.
  • The framework explaining results it has seen and predicting nothing it hasn't.
  • The correspondence measure rewarding elaborate mappings over simple accurate ones — the signature of a machine for producing attractive nonsense.
